Ornithocentrism

It’s that time of year again. Another season characterized by what falls out of the sky. Spring has rain, and winter has snow. Summer does not fit this framework, so for convenience I will ignore it. What I would really like to talk about today, though, is the season so defined by falling that we decided to name it after the very idea.

That is to say, Autumn.

Because to the bird, there is no such thing as falling.

There is the choice to rise, the choice to descend, and on unfortunate occasion, the failure to fly. You might say that failing to fly is, by definition, falling. But a bird would tell you that things are drawn to Earth, who herself is always flying. By extension, all things are always flying all the time; soaring alongside the other planets, moons, and stars. You might have difficulty conceiving of this world without falling, but that’s because where you look up at the night sky and see hunters and bears and a very large spoon, the bird sees a great, innumerable flock. You call it a falling star, but a bird calls it a flying star. Where our Milky Way has arms, theirs has wings.

To further delineate the anthro-ornitho phenomenological crevasse: A bird cannot “fall on hard times” because there is no falling in a bird’s mind. If you ask a bird in dire need about their situation, they will simply tell you they’ve “flown on warmer winds.” Likewise, a bird cannot “fall” into a trap, nor do they have a strong conception of what a “cage” even is. A bird only knows that its wings have been at its side for longer than it would like, even if it cannot explain why the sensation is disconcerting.

Though it may seem sad that to the bird, there is no such thing as “falling” in love, you’ll be relieved to hear they have their own word for this idea. Roughly translated, it means “deciding to fly with another, for as long and far as one can.”

Now, I know what you are thinking: “What of the noble ostrich? Of the mighty cassowary? The enigmatic kiwi? (The oft-forgotten rhea!) Won’t you consider the humble penguin who—though hydrodynamically gifted—must have a perfectly intact concept of falling?”

I will answer by saying they all have their own contemptible biases, each more nonsensical than the last. I will not digress to list them here because, independent of their myriad metaphysical misconceptions, my point still stands:


To the bird, the entire universe is flying.

It just has to be.
