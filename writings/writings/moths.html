On the Design and Ontology of Moths

By the Scribe Unmoored



Lilmothiit astrologers were among the first to derive the alchemical properties of the common hummingbird moth. Among these, their capacity for fortifying those spells associated with summoning Daedra, their deleterious effects on stamina, and the fact that moths do not exist.

It was this fact that inspired the Lilmothiit to engineer perhaps the most obscure, and spectacular, spacecraft ever conceived.

The mothship, or lepidopter, is widely considered to be a technology entirely lost to time. The Imperial College attempted to recover the only known remains of one such vessel from the swamps of Black Marsh, only for the chassis to disintegrate as it was removed. The preservative qualities of various swamp gasses were considered to have kept the remains intact, until naturally the body was removed from its sanctuary. While a regrettable loss to their archaeologists, they were nonetheless able to recover a significant body of scholarship produced by both the Lilmothiit and ancient Saxhleel on the development, construction, and application of lepidopters.

The first record of the mothship’s development arises from the ledger of an ancient, Lilmothiiti silkhouse. A hefty price was levied for a number of silkmoth larvae, the buyer being denoted as a wealthy individual. The record does not demonstrate the buyer’s occupation, but scholars reasonably infer that their wealth may have corresponded to, or had arisen from, significant magical ability. Another record from the same year—an unprecedented and invaluable coincidence in Merethic Era archaeology—indicates that such moths were unsuited to the task of “liminal escape.” This phrase recurs numerous times in later treatises, with this being the earliest known occurrence.

It would be some time before scholars were given insight into the origins of this wonderful technology. Successive records are dated to nearly a century past the earliest, at a time when the Lilmothiit were engaged in a number of brutal civil conflicts. Treatises on the breeding of hummingbird moths indicate they were used in military applications, with the largest known recorded specimens weighing a horse’s equivalent. It was these same treatises which revealed that nearly a hundred years of selective breeding needed to occur in order to accomplish this incredible feat of animal husbandry. The Lilmothiit had begun long before these wars to selectively breed moths for size, with an unwavering emphasis on the dimensions that would later come to define the lepidopter. The largest specimens were eventually co-opted for warfare, igniting a furious race for warring factions to breed the largest, most dutiful, and most aggressive moths possible. But even these ferocious prototypes could not compare to the lepidopter proper, which—according to numerous accounts—were found to be on average the weight of seven horses.

Which brings us to the vessels themselves. Nearly four hundred years after the first recorded mention of liminal escape, the only complete document of a successful mothship journey was recovered. While its content indicates that it was not a unique report, it is the only document of its kind ever recovered. With this text, Imperial scholars were finally able to deduce the mechanics of the lepidopter, its purpose, and its operation.

The Lilmothiit astrologers describe a triploblastic ego-gradient inherent in the physiology of the vessels. Owing to the fact that moths do not exist, lepidopters were able to remain intact when traversing the void, where nonexistence is the medium through which vessels must travel. Their passengers remained unharmed, nestled within the vessel’s entrails.

Surgical anastomoses of the bursa copulatrix and open circulatory system offered an entrance via the ovipositor, implying that only female mothships could be operated by a pilot. Perhaps the most delightful consequences of this limitation were the naming conventions imposed by lepidopter pilots. Names common in lepidopter registries often included the Lilmothiit words for flowers: Lobelia, Milkweed, and Lily being among the most prevalent examples. In the aforementioned documentation of a mothship voyage, the vessel was rather affectionately named the Rose Mallow.”

Indeed, there is compelling evidence to suggest that mothship pilots developed close relationships with their vessels. The record of the Rose Mallow’s voyage indicates she was operated by direct stimulation of the vessel’s nervous system, a technique eerily reminiscent of the Silt-Strider caravaneers of Vvardenfell. Great care was taken in documenting any and all suffering inflicted by the pilot during the journey, implying deliberate care was taken when operating one’s lepidopter. The act of piloting, in fact, was more often described as “being in conversation” with the vessel itself. The steering ganglia were handled only by designated individuals, and often a pilot and his lepidopter operated together for the duration of either being’s life.

In turn, the mothship’s behavior was catalogued in that same document. The aggression of their bygone ancestors was never diminished in the latest models. It seems that mothships were fiercely protective of their handlers—to the point that weapon installations on their carapaces were deemed inadvisable.

Which brings us to the more pragmatic details of the mothship’s design. Not only would weapons have been ill-suited to the creatures’ temperaments, they would have hindered its ability to traverse the void. In fact, all exterior modifications were limited to those that utilized the vessels’ anatomy. The interiors, however, were altered extensively. Standard reorganization of the viscera optimized cabin space, while furniture was installed throughout the abdomen, thorax, and head. The pilot’s chair was situated between the eyes, which served as viewports, while the supraesophageal nerves were altered to provide critical environmental feedback through a series of rigged bells. Astonishingly, mothships were described as possessing two pairs of wings and an associated pair of halteres, a trait possessed by no other known insectoid. The campaniform sensilla associated with these structures modulated the pilot’s control, implying lepidopters were highly maneuverable craft. Internal light was supplied by a series of oil lamps, which collected flowing tallow from the interior walls as fuel. And of course, the vessel’s powerful insectoid legs served as incredibly versatile landing gear.

When compared to today’s voidcraft technologies, the mothship appears to be a charming, if obsolete, design. Though potentially the first craft capable of faster-than-light travel, they were primitive, poorly-armored, cramped, flammable, and unpredictable solutions to liminal escape.

But there is one manuscript, dated to the first recorded instance of the Lilmothiit’s involvement in the Blossom Wars, which beautifully points out:

“Any other ship can keep you safe.

But a moth has personality.

…And a proboscis that can penetrate a Numidic cruiser’s hull.”
