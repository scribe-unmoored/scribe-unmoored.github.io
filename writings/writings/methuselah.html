Methuselah

When the lights went out, when the ever-present hum of atmospheric compensators went quiet, I thought for sure that was the end. It dragged on for what felt like hours. We were all standing in the dark, sitting like children waiting for the damn bus, as though the lights would come back on by magic. I don’t blame us. EMPs were outlawed by intrasolar law, and this was a routine evacuation—but when did the law ever stop people from doing something awful? I remember dragging my leg, thinking with the delusion of a dead man that any moment the damn thing would just flicker back to life.

Others were worse off. A few of us didn’t have arms when it went dark. Corey couldn’t speak anymore, but we were certain he still understood us. I remember Watanabe hitting the floor the second the lights were off. She didn’t get up again, even after it was all over.

Evacuation was only just beginning. We had a dozen or so: employees, janitorial staff, the museum’s curator; all in tow. They were quiet too. Stuff like that’s probably scarier when you don’t have a gun.

Not that our kit was going to do much for us at that point. HUDs were fried. My QBZ-28 wasn’t cooperating. I tried to restart the targeting matrix. All I got in return was a spastic gyro and a face-full of battery smoke. I threw the thing aside before it had a chance to blow up in my face.

We were passing out whatever we had left. The occasional AR or sidearm with pulse shielding (expensive stuff—the kind we were shocked to see some of our company toting), knives, and the medic Tom’s old semi-auto. “Less points of failure,” he always used to say. Good thing it was dark. The smirk on his face must have been insufferable.

Altogether we had about one gun for every four of us—not counting the poor sods who couldn’t pull a trigger, or didn’t know how. Two crawlers packing 25mm, enough ammunition to log a Jupiter oak—all of it useless once our gear was fried.

Thought it was bad when I realized we’d all suffocate to death in a few hours. Quiet and in the dark. Not the worst way to go. But a damn disappointing one.

Never expected the lights coming back on to somehow be worse.

It was floodlights really, pouring in through the museum’s windows. A VTOL, buzzing around outside. At first you could tell there was some relief, especially among the civvies—then that’s when it all started to sink in, for all of us. There was this sound, everyone who’s seen combat knows it: that radio garble your HUD AI generates when you’re shouting orders to someone. Supposed to hide what you’re saying from anyone not keyed into your channel. Sounds damn near inhuman when you hear it in the dark. Especially when you know it’s not one of yours.

Museum curator said something about weapons. The museum had plenty to commandeer. We pushed further in looking, past a dozen or so displays of old hollowed-out stardoggers and old Earth tanks. Everything that might have still had a ghost in it was fried, and anything too dumb to be fried we naturally didn’t have the ammunition for.

We set up in a 23rd century installation—a little cul de sac where we at the least had a choke point and a steel corpse on either flank. I was always a bit of a history dork. This was Martian War stuff—one of my favorites when I was a kid. Probably why I ended up in this line of work in the first place. Made peace with dying quick and ugly a long time ago. figured it would be fitting to do it next to the same bunch of 200-year-old antiques I used to dream about.

Was eyeing up a loader, sort of thing you’d see on the glacierbreakers back in the day, when something came back to me. Sort of thing you think about when you’re about to die, I guess. Childhood obsessions.

I asked the curator, “You got a Moose?”

“A Moose?” Can’t blame him. Probably wasn’t thinking too much about curating in the moment.

“M12a—we’re in the Martian War right? Judging by that loader I’m guessing Battle of Olympus Mons—2232?”

I remember the captain telling me to shut the hell up. Staying quiet was probably helping us stay alive a few extra seconds. I asked about the Moose again.

“I—right over there,”

Curator pointed somewhere behind me. Felt like an idiot, but it was dark and I was scared out of my mind. Damn thing was standing right over us. Six tons and nearly twenty feet of industrial hydraulic scaffold towering over us. It only took staring at the dark for awhile to make out the chassis. Was so close I couldn’t see the mech for the machine.

So I did probably the stupidest thing I’ve ever done in my entire life. Started dragging my leg across the floor, making enough noise to rouse a sleeping stone. And then I started climbing.

“What the hell are you doing, lieutenant?” Was among one of the more tame things my squad started shouting at me. I knew if I fell that was probably it. Wouldn’t be time to get back up before they were on us and gunning us down. Yelled for them to feed in the 25mm. Captain told us to lug it along in case we found something we could use—well I figured we could use this.

Could hear that garbled noise coming from everywhere now. Like a bunch of demons coming out of the dark. Was in the cockpit when I heard their footsteps, even from twenty something feet up. Tom shot something that came in, or he missed and told them exactly where we were, if they didn’t know already by that point.

Captain was screaming his head off. One of them janitor types was sobbing. Someone had the head to feed the 25mm, but just about everyone was telling me to do the only sensible thing they could see: get the hell down and help us.

What they didn’t know was that the M12a Moose was an old loader, repurposed early on when the rebels were losing the war. They took this exosuit that let a man fold a dime between his fingers or lift a tank, and they decided it needed a rotating cannon on one arm, and a bigass bayonet on the other.

And you know the thing about those industrial machines. All low tech. The Moose had a manual ignition override. Hydraulics managed most of the joint movements. No onboard computers. Just a hell of a lot of physics.

But most importantly, they had those old internal combustion engines. Practically paleolithic.

The bastards ran on gasoline.

Which was so obsolete by even the 22nd century, that it was the sort of thing you’d only ever see in a museum.

Took three heavy kicks to knock the ignition into place. Starter squealed at first, loud enough to hurt your ears. Couldn’t hear anything inside the cockpit. Made me think if they started shooting, I wouldn’t even know it. Then the engine turned over, like a thylacat howling. I was coughing up whatever black crud it belched out. Heat started to cook me almost instantly. But then I could feel it. The whole thing, like something alive. Your modern exosuits, they’re all quiet. Composite nuclear and solar. Sitting in a Moose was like riding an earthquake—and I’ll never forget that sound.

Chugchugchugchugchugchugchug.

Didn’t think it could shake any harder than it was without falling apart. Then the cannon spooled. Don’t know if you’ve ever heard a Venusian orchestra before. I haven’t. Guessing it was something like those two noises coming together.

Floodlights came on one at a time. I remember how satisfying that soft click was. Incandescent. Real old school stuff.

In front of it, there was a little velvet rope and a sign that said “NO CLIMBING.”

I like to think that’s the last thing someone saw before the Moose turned them to paste.
